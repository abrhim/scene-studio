Lawrence Himmer

CAPTAIN · USMC · CO, COMPANY C, 1ST BN, 9TH MARINES · 23 JUL 1938 — 16 APR 1968 · THE WALL, PANEL 50E · LINE 14

Husband of Sharon. Father of five — the youngest born six weeks after he fell. Killed leading his company up a nameless hill southwest of Khe Sanh, going to the relief of other Marines.

“Was with Charlie Company the day the Skipper died. Will never forget.” — Jim Thompson, C/1/9, remembrance left at the Wall of Faces, 2020 [4]

Sheet I · The Man

Larry

Lawrence “Larry” Himmer was 29, from Chula Vista, California, on his third tour in Vietnam.[4] Family remembrances describe a gifted artist and an easy people-person — loving, considerate, quick with an encouraging word — an active Latter-day Saint who held the office of Elder, devoted to his parents and siblings.[4] He wrote home to his four children — Richard, Dale, Teresa, and LaShel — telling them to mind their mother, Sharon, who was expecting. Jennifer Lana Himmer was born May 31, 1968, six weeks after her father was killed.[4]

His ribbon set includes the Good Conduct Medal — an award only enlisted Marines can earn — which points to a start in the ranks and a commission earned later: what the Corps calls a Mustang.[5] It fits the man the records show: a 29-year-old captain, three tours deep, commanding a rifle company. Before his last tour, the family recalls, he left with his affairs in order.[4]

Sheet II · The Battalion

The Walking Dead

1st Battalion, 9th Marines carried the highest killed-in-action rate of any Marine battalion in history: of 2,892 Marines and corpsmen who served in it across 47 months in Vietnam, 747 were killed — 25.89 percent.[15] Through the first months of 1968 the battalion helped hold Khe Sanh Combat Base through the 77-day siege, dug in at the Rock Quarry west of the base under fire that ran to hundreds of incoming rounds a day.[1]

The siege was lifted by Operation Pegasus in early April. In its closing days 1/9 went back on the offensive, taking Hill 552 on 7 April and Hill 689 on 8 April against accurate mortar fire.[1] On 15 April 1968 at 0800, Operation Scotland II opened — the Marines pushing out to clear the hills the NVA had held around the base.[7] 1/9, under LtCol John “Blackie” Cahill (in command since 1 April), set its battalion perimeter and operations center on Hill 689.[1][3]

Sheet III · The Ground

Two miles west of Khe Sanh

Everything on this sheet sits within four miles. Khe Sanh Combat Base on its plateau; the Rock Quarry at its western edge, 1/9’s home through the siege; Hill 552 and Hill 689 on the ridge running west — both taken by 1/9 in early April; and, about a mile southwest of Hill 689, the unnamed elephant-grass ridge the official history calls only “a small adjoining hill.” The Marines who fought there knew it by its map elevation, Hill 600 — and afterward as Dead Man’s Hill.[1][3]

Hill 689 was where 1/9 lived — the perimeter, the CP, the ops center. The battle of 16 April was fought on the other hill, a mile to its southwest.[1][3]

Sheet IV · The Battle

16 April 1968

That morning, Army air-cavalry scouts finishing Operation Pegasus spotted what the Marines most needed to know: all elements of the NVA 66th Regiment, 304th Division, had moved back into the hills near Route 9 and Lang Vei. The report never reached Marine commanders.[3] So when Company A stepped off its patrol at 0700, battalion believed the ridge a mile southwest held perhaps a squad. It held a fortified complex of a regiment that had been digging for weeks.

Daily journal · reconstructed · 16–17 April 1968
AM

Army air cavalry sights elements of the NVA 66th Regiment, 304th Division, back in the hills near Route 9 and Lang Vei. The report is never passed to Marine commanders.[3]

0700

Capt Henry D. Banks leads two reinforced platoons of Company A southwest off the Hill 689 perimeter.[2]

1000

Company A halts; two squads push onto a ridge in four-to-six-foot elephant grass and take small-arms and mortar fire from concealed bunkers. The first Marines fall.[2]

~1030

1stLt Michael P. Hayden’s 2d Platoon assaults; Hayden and then his platoon sergeant are killed in rapid succession. Alpha is pinned on the north end of the ridge — 10 dead, 20 wounded. Banks twice declines offered help, believing he faces a squad.[2]

~1200

Banks reverses and asks for relief.[2]

1320

LtCol Cahill commits Companies C and D. (His message doesn’t reach the regimental commander, Col Meyers, until 1543 — the radio-relay fog of that day.)[2]

~1400

Two platoons of Company C move out first — Capt Himmer leading, Cahill accompanying — and climb roughly 1,800 feet of the hill’s height toward the fight.[2][3]

~1430

Finding Alpha immobile on the north end, Cahill “ordered Himmer to attack from the south” — the veteran account has Charlie moving onto the eastern-southeastern ridge — while Delta presses from the northwest.[2][3]

PM

The relief attack bogs down in crossfire from bunkers, spider holes, and B-52 craters. Alpha’s dead and wounded are withdrawn. Somewhere on this southern approach, Captain Lawrence Himmer is killed leading his company. One of his Marines, PFC David Dee Brown Jr — four days in country — falls on the southern side of the hill.[3][13]

1745

Cahill orders every man off the hill with the wounded. The dead stay behind — “We will get them later.”[3]

night–0400

Entangled with the NVA in the dark, the companies work free and fall back northeast to Hill 689, completing the withdrawal in the early morning of 17 April.[1][7]

Official-history figures.[1][7] A veterans’ memorial page gives differing tallies for 16–19 April (9 KIA / 46 WIA / 36 MIA), likely reflecting confused initial field reporting; the official figures are treated as authoritative.[8]

Roughly one man in five who went up those slopes became a casualty in a single afternoon — among the worst days any Marine battalion endured in 1968. The battalion commander, LtCol John “Blackie” Cahill, was relieved of duty afterward; the roster shows his command passing to LtCol James W. Quinn on 13 May.[1][7] The official history states no reason. The record cuts both ways: he declined offered reinforcements and fed companies into a fortified position — yet the intelligence that would have changed everything was withheld above his level, and his caution about recovering the dead was echoed days later by regiment and division, who themselves suspended rescue attempts as too costly.[2][3] Cahill — Chosin Reservoir veteran, Silver Star, four Purple Hearts — retired a colonel and died in 2005.[9]

Sheet VI · Coming Home

Fort Rosecrans, Point Loma

Captain Himmer was brought home to San Diego and buried at Fort Rosecrans National Cemetery, Plot Q 1222, above the water at Point Loma — a few miles from Chula Vista.[14] His decorations include the Purple Heart, Combat Action Ribbon, Good Conduct Medal, National Defense Service Medal, Vietnam Service Medal, and Vietnam Campaign Medal.[5] His name is cut into Panel 50E, Line 14 of the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, among the men of the 16th of April.[4]

Six weeks after he fell, Jennifer was born. Fifty-two years after that, a Marine who had been on the hill found his page on the Wall of Faces and left eleven words about the Skipper. His company remembered him. His family still does.

Reliability notes

Sourced facts above trace to the official history, the memorial databases, or period records. Derived: the battle hill’s position (≈ XD 792 398) is plotted one mile southwest of Hill 689 per the veteran account — not read from a period grid; the real 6342-III sheet would refine it. Approximate: Hill 552, the Rock Quarry, Khe Sanh village, Delta’s start point, and the medevac LZ are placed from narrative relationships. Unit routes are schematic — faithful to the documented sequence and directions, not to a surveyed trace. The name “Hill 600” is the Marines’ field designation; the official history leaves the ridge unnumbered. Casualty figures vary between sources as flagged in Sheet V. Larry’s role as C Company’s commanding officer rests on the official history’s account of Cahill’s order to him, his rank, and a Charlie Company veteran’s “Skipper” remembrance; the April 1968 command chronology’s staff roster would confirm it beyond doubt.
